Chapter 6 Searching sequences with BLAST

In this chapter

  • why genes can be read in six frames, and what that means for searching
  • local vs. global alignment
  • how BLAST finds and scores matches, and what the E-value means
  • the five main BLAST programs, and which one to use
  • how to build a BLAST database and search it from the command line
  • how to read a BLAST report, and how to get tabular output you can filter
  • how to run many searches at once, on your computer or on the cluster

BLAST (Basic Local Alignment Search Tool) finds sequences that are similar to the one you have. It’s probably the single most used program in bioinformatics: the original paper (Altschul et al. 1990) is one of the most cited scientific papers of all time. Whenever you ask “What is this sequence?”, “Where is this gene in my genome?”, or “Does this species have a copy of that gene?”, BLAST is usually the first tool you reach for.

Most people first meet BLAST on NCBI’s website, which is great for searching one sequence against GenBank. This chapter is about running BLAST yourself, at the command line, which lets you:

  • search your own data, like a new genome assembly that isn’t in GenBank yet
  • run thousands of searches at once, on your computer or on the cluster
  • get results in a table you can filter with the tools from Chapter 1
  • record exactly what you did, so you can do it again (Section 3.5)

6.1 Background: strands and reading frames

DNA is double stranded, and the two strands run in opposite directions, 5’ to 3’. Genes can be encoded on either strand. A gene on the “top” strand of a genome sequence reads left to right; a gene on the “bottom” strand reads right to left, on the reverse complement.

The genetic code is read three bases (one codon) at a time, so any DNA sequence can be translated in three different reading frames, depending on whether you start at the first, second, or third base. With two strands, that’s six frames. Here are the six translations of a short sequence (* is a stop codon):

5' CCTCTCGAATGGTAA 3'    top strand
3' GGAGAGCTTACCATT 5'    bottom strand

Frame   Codons                    Protein
+1      CCT CTC GAA TGG TAA       P L E W *
+2       CTC TCG AAT GGT          L S N G
+3        TCT CGA ATG GTA         S R M V
-1      TTA CCA TTC GAG AGG       L P F E R       (reverse complement:
-2       TAC CAT TCG AGA          Y H S R          5' TTACCATTCGAGAGG 3')
-3        ACC ATT CGA GAG         T I R E

Usually, only one of the six frames codes for a real protein. When BLAST compares DNA to protein, it translates the DNA in all six frames and tries each one. It reports the frame of each match, so the frame (or strand) also tells you which way the gene runs in the genome.

6.2 Local vs. global alignment

BLAST finds matches by aligning sequences, lining them up to maximize their similarity. There are two kinds of alignment:

  • A global alignment lines up two sequences across their entire length. It answers “How similar are these two sequences, end to end?”
  • A local alignment finds the most similar parts of two sequences, and ignores the rest. It answers “Which pieces of these sequences match?”

An analogy: a local alignment finds the two most similar sentences in a pair of books; a global alignment compares two sentences word for word, from start to finish.

Global alignment makes sense when the sequences are related along their whole length, like copies of the same gene from different species (it’s what programs for multiple sequence alignment do). But genomes are mostly noncoding DNA that isn’t shared between species, so global alignments of whole genomes rarely make sense. We usually want local alignments:

  • finding a gene, or a conserved region, in a genome
  • mapping a transcript (mRNA) back to the genome it came from
  • finding a conserved domain within a protein

BLAST does local alignment. Each match it finds is called a high-scoring segment pair, or HSP. One query can match several places in the same subject sequence, so one hit can have several HSPs.

6.3 How BLAST works

Comparing a query to every position in a big database with a full alignment algorithm is too slow. BLAST takes a shortcut that’s much faster, at the cost of sometimes missing weak matches. It works in three steps:

  1. Seed. Chop the query into short overlapping “words” and make a lookup table of them. Scan the database for words that match. For proteins, “match” includes similar words, not just identical ones.
  2. Extend. When a word matches, try to extend the alignment in both directions, keeping track of the score as you go. Stop when the score drops too far below the best score seen so far.
  3. Evaluate. Keep the extended alignments that score well enough to be unlikely to be due to chance, and report them from best to worst.

6.3.1 Scoring alignments

The score of an alignment adds up rewards and penalties:

  • a reward for each match
  • a penalty for each mismatch
  • a penalty for opening a gap, and a smaller one for each position the gap is extended

For example, these two sequences:

CCTCTCGAGAGTTCT
GCTCTTGGTCT

align better with a gap than without one:

CCTCTCGAGAGTTCT
GCTCTTG----GTCT

You can change all of these values. Higher penalties make BLAST more stringent; lower ones let it find more distant matches (along with more junk).

6.3.2 Substitution matrices for proteins

DNA has only four bases, so a simple match/mismatch score works. Proteins are different. There are 20 amino acids, and some substitutions are much more common than others. Swapping one small, hydrophobic amino acid for another, like isoleucine (I) for valine (V), often doesn’t change a protein much, so natural selection allows it. Swapping for tryptophan (W) usually isn’t tolerated.

Protein searches therefore score each pair of amino acids with a substitution matrix, built from how often each substitution is seen in real, aligned proteins. The default is BLOSUM62 (Henikoff & Henikoff 1992). Here it is (from NCBI’s copy of the matrix):

Rare amino acids, like tryptophan (W, 11) and cysteine (C, 9), score highest when they match, because a match is least likely to be a coincidence. Notice that I and V score +3: a mismatch, but a “positive” one. BLAST reports these as positives.

6.4 The five BLAST programs

Which program you use depends on what kind of sequence you have (the query) and what kind of sequences you’re searching (the database, or subject sequences):

Program Query Database Typical use
blastn nucleotide nucleotide find a gene, or any DNA sequence, in a genome
blastp protein protein find related proteins
blastx translated nucleotide protein what proteins might this DNA sequence (a contig, a transcript) encode?
tblastn protein translated nucleotide find genes for a protein in an unannotated genome
tblastx translated nucleotide translated nucleotide compare distantly related DNA sequences at the protein level (slow)

“Translated” means BLAST translates the sequence in all six reading frames (Section 6.1) before it searches.

Proteins find more distant relatives than DNA. The genetic code is redundant, so DNA sequences change faster than the proteins they encode, and a protein’s sequence holds onto its similarity for much longer. If you’re looking for a gene in a distantly related species, search with the protein (tblastn), not the DNA (blastn).

6.5 Scores and E-values

BLAST gives every match two numbers that tell you how good it is.

  • The score measures the alignment itself. The raw score is the sum of the rewards and penalties (Section 6.3). The bit score rescales the raw score so it no longer depends on the scoring system you used, which lets you compare scores from different searches. Bigger is better.
  • The E-value (expect value) is the number of matches at least this good that you’d expect to find by chance, in a search of this size. Smaller is better. An E-value of 1e-6 (0.000001) means that, in a database this size, you’d expect a random match this good about once in a million searches. An E-value of 5 means you’d expect five matches like this by chance: it’s noise.

The E-value depends on the bit score, S, and on the size of the search, which is roughly the length of the query, m, times the size of the database, n:

\[E \approx m \, n \, 2^{-S}\]

Two things follow from this equation. First, E-values drop exponentially as the score goes up, so a slightly better alignment can have a much smaller E-value. Second, the same alignment gets a larger (worse) E-value in a bigger database, because a big database gives chance more opportunities. You’ll see both of these happen in Section 6.9.

Exercise 6.1 Think about how searches behave before you run one.

  1. Do you expect more hits with very stringent or very relaxed mismatch penalties?
  2. You search the same query against two databases, one with 10 sequences and one with 106 (a million). Which do you expect to give more hits?
  3. You search two queries, 10 and 106 nucleotides long, against the same database. Which do you expect to have more hits?
Answers
  1. Relaxed penalties. Alignments with lots of mismatches still score well, so more of them pass.
  2. The bigger database. There are more sequences to match, both real relatives and chance matches.
  3. The longer query. A million nucleotides contain many more “words” that could match something.

Similarity is not homology. BLAST measures similarity: how alike two sequences are. Homology means two sequences share a common ancestor. Homology is a conclusion you draw, partly from the evidence of similarity. Two sequences are either homologous or not; they can’t be “85% homologous”. A very significant BLAST hit is strong evidence of homology, but short or low-complexity matches can be similar by chance, and distant homologs may not be detectably similar at all.

6.5.1 Setting a threshold

In practice, you decide which hits to believe by setting a cutoff on the E-value or the bit score. There’s no universal right answer, and published papers use a range of cutoffs: 1e-5 and 1e-10 are common for genes and proteins. Good practice is to look at how your results change across a range of cutoffs, and to report the cutoff you used in your methods, so others can reproduce your results. BLAST’s default cutoff is an E-value of 10, which is deliberately generous: it shows you the weak matches, too, and leaves the decision to you.

6.6 Running BLAST at the command line

6.6.1 Getting BLAST

The BLAST programs from NCBI are called BLAST+ (Camacho et al. 2009). There are several ways to get them:

  • On the cluster, BLAST+ is already installed as a module (Section 4.6): module load blast.
  • With a package manager. With conda, conda install -c conda-forge -c bioconda blast. With Homebrew on a Mac, brew install blast.
  • From NCBI. Download a ready-made version for your operating system from NCBI’s FTP site. Check its checksum (Section 4.5), unpack it, and add its bin directory to your PATH, or link the programs into ~/local/bin (Section 2.1.6).

BLAST+ is a collection of programs: the five search programs, plus makeblastdb for building databases and several others. Check that it works, and which version you have:

blastn -version
blastn: 2.7.1+
 Package: blast 2.7.1, build Oct 18 2017 20:40:57

Record the version. BLAST’s defaults have changed over the years, so the same search can give slightly different results in different versions. Write the version you used in your project notebook (Section 3.5.2). At the time of writing, the newest version is 2.17.0.

Every BLAST program has dozens of options. To see them all:

blastn -help | less

6.6.2 Step 1: make a database

A local BLAST search has two steps: format a database of the sequences you want to search, then search it. The database is built from a FASTA file with one or more sequences, either nucleotides or proteins, but not both. In the course files, watermelon_genome/watermelon.fsa is the watermelon mitochondrial genome. Make a nucleotide database from it with makeblastdb:

makeblastdb -in watermelon.fsa -dbtype nucl

makeblastdb prints a short report and writes several index files next to the FASTA file:

ls watermelon.fsa*
watermelon.fsa
watermelon.fsa.nhr
watermelon.fsa.nin
watermelon.fsa.nsq

The .n files are for a nucleotide database; protein databases get .p files. (Newer versions of BLAST+ make a few more.) You don’t need to look inside them, and you can always make them again from the FASTA file. To use the database, give BLAST its name, watermelon.fsa, not the names of the index files.

Option What it does
-in the input FASTA file
-dbtype nucl or prot
-out a name for the database (default: the name of the input file)
-title a description of the database, shown in BLAST reports
-parse_seqids use the FASTA IDs as sequence IDs, so you can pull sequences back out with blastdbcmd

6.6.4 Reading a BLAST report

A BLAST report has four parts.

1. The header. The program and version, a reference to cite, and a description of the database (1 sequences; 379,236 total letters) and the query (Query= Citrullus_nad4L, Length=303).

2. The one-line summaries. One line per database sequence that has a significant match, best first, with the bit score and E-value of its best alignment.

3. The alignments. Each database sequence that matched (the subject, abbreviated Sbjct) starts with > and its description. Then come its HSPs, each with:

  • Score: the bit score, with the raw score in parentheses
  • Expect: the E-value
  • Identities: how many positions are identical, out of the length of the alignment
  • Gaps: how many positions are gaps
  • Strand: which strands of the query and subject match. Plus/Minus means the query matches the reverse complement of the subject. nad4L is on the bottom strand of the genome, so the subject coordinates count down (366432 to 366130) while the query coordinates count up.
  • the alignment itself, in rows of 60, with the start and end coordinates of each row. A | marks an identical position.

4. The footer. The statistical parameters BLAST used: λ and K (constants that depend on the scoring system, used to calculate bit scores and E-values), the effective size of the search space (m × n, adjusted for edge effects), and the scoring settings (Matrix: blastn matrix 1 -2 is a reward of 1 and a penalty of −2).

6.7 Tabular output

BLAST reports are made for people to read. When you have many results, you want a table instead, one line per HSP, that you can sort, filter, and count with the tools from Chapter 1. Use -outfmt 6 for tab-separated columns:

blastn -query nt/cox1.fasta -db watermelon.fsa -outfmt 6
Citrullus_cox1  watermelon  100.000 858 0   0   727 1584    1694    2551    0.0 1585
Citrullus_cox1  watermelon  100.000 726 0   0   1   726 1   726 0.0 1341

6.7.1 The 12 columns of -outfmt 6

-outfmt 6 is probably the most used BLAST output format, but the table has no header, so you need to know what the columns are. Each line is one HSP (Section 6.2), and the 12 columns are always in this order:

Column Name Meaning
1 qseqid query sequence ID: the first word of the query’s FASTA header
2 sseqid subject sequence ID: the first word of the matching database sequence’s header
3 pident percent identity: identical positions ÷ alignment length × 100
4 length alignment length, including gaps
5 mismatch number of mismatched positions
6 gapopen number of gap openings (one gap of 5 positions counts once)
7 qstart start of the alignment in the query
8 qend end of the alignment in the query
9 sstart start of the alignment in the subject
10 send end of the alignment in the subject
11 evalue E-value (Section 6.5); smaller is better
12 bitscore bit score; bigger is better

A few things to watch for:

  • Strand. If sstart is bigger than send, the query matches the subject’s reverse complement (the bottom strand). qstart is always smaller than qend in blastn and tblastn output.
  • Several lines per subject. One query can have several HSPs in the same subject, like the two exons of cox1 above, so a subject ID can appear on several lines.
  • Percent identity is over the aligned part only. A 30 bp alignment at 100% identity can be a much weaker match than a 1,500 bp alignment at 95%. Look at length (or qcovs, below) and the E-value, not just pident.
  • Coordinates start at 1 and include both ends, so an alignment from 1 to 726 covers 726 positions.

-outfmt 7 is the same table with comment lines that name the columns, which is handy for checking:

blastn -query nt/cox1.fasta -db watermelon.fsa -outfmt 7
# BLASTN 2.7.1+
# Query: Citrullus_cox1
# Database: watermelon.fsa
# Fields: query acc.ver, subject acc.ver, % identity, alignment length, mismatches, gap opens, q. start, q. end, s. start, s. end, evalue, bit score
# 2 hits found
Citrullus_cox1  watermelon  100.000 858 0   0   727 1584    1694    2551    0.0 1585
Citrullus_cox1  watermelon  100.000 726 0   0   1   726 1   726 0.0 1341
# BLAST processed 1 queries

6.7.2 Choosing your own columns

You can also choose your own columns, in any order. Put 6 and the column names in quotes, so they’re one argument:

blastn -query nt/cox1.fasta -db watermelon.fsa -outfmt "6 qseqid qstart qend sstart send evalue"
Citrullus_cox1  727 1584    1694    2551    0.0
Citrullus_cox1  1   726 1   726 0.0

Other useful columns include qlen and slen (sequence lengths), qcovs (how much of the query is covered by alignments to this subject), stitle (the subject’s full description), and sframe or qframe (the reading frame of a translated search). blastn -help lists them all, under -outfmt.

Tables work with everything you already know. For example, to keep only hits with an E-value below 1e-10, use awk -F '\t' '$11 < 1e-10' (Section 1.13). To list the subjects, cut -f 2 | sort -u. And to read a table in R, read.table("results.blastn", sep = "\t").

Other formats include -outfmt 5 (XML) and -outfmt 15 (JSON), for other programs to read, and -outfmt 0, the default report.

6.8 Searching with proteins

The aa directory has the protein sequences of the same watermelon genes. To find a protein-coding gene in a genome, search the genome with its protein with tblastn. The same database works, because tblastn translates the genome in all six frames as it searches. Add sframe to the columns to see which frame matched:

tblastn -query aa/nad4L.fasta -db watermelon.fsa -outfmt "6 qseqid sseqid pident length sstart send evalue sframe"
Citrullus_lanatus_nad4L watermelon  100.000 99  366429  366133  3.01e-36    -1

The match is in frame −1: the bottom strand, as blastn found earlier.

6.8.1 Searching a single sequence, without a database

To compare your query to just one or a few sequences, you don’t need a database. Give the sequences to -subject instead of -db. Here’s the watermelon NAD4L protein against the mitochondrial genome of Cycas, a distantly related seed plant. Lambda      K        H        a         alpha

Things to notice in a protein alignment:

  • Identities and positives. The middle line shows the amino acid for identical positions and a + for mismatches that score positively in BLOSUM62 (Section 6.3), like I for V.
  • Frame. The match is on the bottom strand (frame −3), so the subject coordinates count down.
  • Lowercase letters. Some of the query is printed in lowercase. These are low-complexity regions, stretches made mostly of a few kinds of amino acids (here, rich in I, L, S, and V). Matches between low-complexity regions are common and usually meaningless, so BLAST masks them: it won’t start a match in a lowercase region, though an alignment can extend into one. For proteins, the masking program is called SEG; for DNA, it’s DUST.
  • Compositional matrix adjust. BLAST adjusted the scores for the amino acid composition of these particular sequences, which makes the E-values more accurate.

6.9 Choosing a search: -task

blastn has several modes, chosen with -task. They differ mostly in the size of the seed words (Section 6.3) and the scoring system:

Task Word size Use it for
megablast (the default) 28 very similar sequences: the same species, or close relatives
dc-megablast 11 more distant relatives (“discontiguous” megablast)
blastn 11 more distant relatives, and short queries
blastn-short 7 very short queries, like primers (under about 30 bp)

The default, megablast, is fast but needs 28 identical bases in a row to start a match. That’s fine within a species, but it misses short or more divergent matches. To see the difference, make a database of all 23 plant mitochondrial genomes in mt_genomes. Instead of combining them into a file first, you can pipe them straight into makeblastdb (-in defaults to STDIN), naming the database with -out:

cat mt_genomes/*.fasta | makeblastdb -dbtype nucl -out mt -title "plant mitochondrial genomes"

Now search for the watermelon trnG(GCC) gene, a 72 bp transfer RNA, and count how many genomes it matches with each task:

blastn -query "nt/trnG(GCC)-1.fasta" -db mt -outfmt 6 | cut -f 2 | sort -u | wc -l
blastn -task blastn -query "nt/trnG(GCC)-1.fasta" -db mt -outfmt 6 | cut -f 2 | sort -u | wc -l
      11
      21

6.10 Searching many sequences at once

6.10.1 One file with many queries

A query file can hold many sequences. BLAST searches each one in turn, and -outfmt 6 puts the query ID in the first column, so all the results can go in one table. Here are all 60 watermelon genes against the genome, counting the hits for each gene and printing the genes with the most hits:

cat nt/*.fasta > all_genes.fasta
blastn -query all_genes.fasta -db watermelon.fsa -outfmt 6 > all_genes.vs.watermelon.blastn
cut -f 1 all_genes.vs.watermelon.blastn | sort | uniq -c | sort -rn | head -n 5
   7 Citrullus_nad2
   6 Citrullus_nad1
   5 Citrullus_nad7
   4 Citrullus_nad5
   4 Citrullus_nad4

These genes are split into several exons, as cox1 was. Several nad genes in plant mitochondria are even assembled from pieces encoded in different parts of the genome.

Check the end of your FASTA files. If the last line of a file doesn’t end with a newline, cat joins it to the first line of the next file, so the next header ends up in the middle of a sequence (...TTTCATTGA>Citrullus_nad4L). BLAST warns about “invalid residues” and searches the two genes as one. You can check a file’s last character with tail -c 1 file | od -c, which prints \n if it ends with a newline.

6.10.2 A loop, with one output file per query

To keep the results for each gene in their own file, use a loop (Section 1.16), with parameter expansion (Section 1.16.2) to make the output file names:

mkdir -p blast_out
for gene in nt/*.fasta; do
  name=${gene#nt/}               # nt/atp1.fasta -> atp1.fasta
  blastn -query "$gene" -db watermelon.fsa -outfmt 6 > "blast_out/${name%.fasta}.blastn"
done
ls blast_out | head -n 4
atp1.blastn
atp4.blastn
atp6.blastn
atp8.blastn

Print the commands before you run them. Put echo in front of the command in a loop, with the whole command in double quotes, and the loop prints each command instead of running it. The > is inside the quotes, so it’s printed rather than redirecting echo. The single quotes around the file names will protect names like trnG(GCC)-1.fasta when the commands run:

for gene in nt/trn*.fasta; do
  name=${gene#nt/}
  echo "blastn -query '$gene' -db watermelon.fsa -outfmt 6 > 'blast_out/${name%.fasta}.blastn'"
done | head -n 3
blastn -query 'nt/trnC(GCA)-1.fasta' -db watermelon.fsa -outfmt 6 > 'blast_out/trnC(GCA)-1.blastn'
blastn -query 'nt/trnC(GCA)-2.fasta' -db watermelon.fsa -outfmt 6 > 'blast_out/trnC(GCA)-2.blastn'
blastn -query 'nt/trnE(TTC).fasta' -db watermelon.fsa -outfmt 6 > 'blast_out/trnE(TTC).blastn'

If the commands look right, remove the echo and the quotes around the command. Or redirect the loop’s output to a file, > blast_commands.sh, and you have a script that runs every search, ready to run with bash blast_commands.sh or from a job on the cluster.

6.11 BLAST on the cluster

Big searches belong on the cluster (Chapter 4). BLAST can use several processor cores at once with -num_threads; set it to the number of cores you request from SLURM. Here’s a job script (Section 4.6) that builds the database and runs a script of BLAST commands, like the one from the previous section, on the scratch disk (Section 4.6.2):

#!/bin/bash
#SBATCH --job-name=all.blast
#SBATCH --partition comp01
#SBATCH --qos comp
#SBATCH --nodes=1
#SBATCH --cpus-per-task=32
#SBATCH --time=01:00:00
#SBATCH -o %x.%j.out
#SBATCH -e %x.%j.err

module purge
module load blast

cd $SLURM_SUBMIT_DIR

# copy the input files to the scratch disk, and work there
cp -r nt watermelon.fsa blast_commands.sh /scratch/$SLURM_JOB_ID/
cd /scratch/$SLURM_JOB_ID

makeblastdb -in watermelon.fsa -dbtype nucl
bash blast_commands.sh

# copy the results back
rsync -a /scratch/$SLURM_JOB_ID/ $SLURM_SUBMIT_DIR/

For this script, make blast_commands.sh with -num_threads 32 in each command. files/other_files/all-blastn.slurm and atp9-blastn.slurm in the course files are scripts like this one.

6.12 Useful options

Option What it does
-query file the query sequence(s), in FASTA format
-db name the database to search
-subject file search these sequences instead of a database
-out file write the output to a file
-outfmt 6 tabular output (7 adds comments; "6 col1 col2 ..." picks the columns)
-evalue 1e-10 report only hits with E-values below this (default: 10)
-task blastn the search mode (Section 6.9)
-num_threads 8 use 8 processor cores
-word_size, -reward, -penalty, -gapopen, -gapextend adjust the seeding and scoring
-dust no, -seg no turn off low-complexity masking (nucleotide, protein)
-max_target_seqs 500 the maximum number of subject sequences to report (see below)
-remote search NCBI’s databases (like -db nt) over the internet, instead of a local one

-max_target_seqs doesn’t mean “the best N hits”. It’s tempting to use -max_target_seqs 1 to get the best hit for each query, but BLAST applies the limit during the search, not after, so the hit you get isn’t always the best one (Shah et al. 2019). Keep the default, and pick the best hit from the results yourself, for example with sort.

6.13 Beyond BLAST

BLAST is the standard, but it’s not the only tool, or the fastest:

  • DIAMOND and MMseqs2 do protein searches (blastp and blastx) hundreds or thousands of times faster than BLAST, with similar sensitivity (Buchfink et al. 2021; Steinegger & Söding 2017). They’re the usual choice for huge searches, like annotating all the genes in a metagenome.
  • To map sequencing reads to a genome, use a read aligner (like BWA, Bowtie2, or minimap2), not BLAST. They’re built for millions of short or long reads.
  • To align whole genomes to each other, use a genome aligner like minimap2 or MUMmer.

The ideas in this chapter (local alignment, seeds, scores, and E-values) carry over to all of them.
